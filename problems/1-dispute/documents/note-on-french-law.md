# Internal know-how note - limitation clauses under French law

*A short in-house note kept by the firm's commercial disputes team. Not legal
advice on any particular matter.*

**Freedom of contract, with limits.** French law generally upholds negotiated
limitation and exclusion clauses between commercial parties (professionnels).
The starting point is that the clause binds.

**Article 1170 of the Code civil.** A clause which deprives a debtor's essential
obligation (*obligation essentielle*) of its substance is deemed unwritten
(*réputée non écrite*). This codifies the *Chronopost* line of cases. The test is
whether the clause, in practice, means the party owes nothing meaningful for
failing at the very thing it was engaged to do. A cap that still leaves a real
remedy is usually safe. A cap or exclusion that leaves the customer with
effectively nothing for total failure of the core obligation is vulnerable.

**Article 1171.** In a contract of adhesion (standard terms not negotiated),
a clause creating a significant imbalance between the parties' rights is deemed
unwritten. This does not apply to clauses on the price or the main subject matter,
and does not apply where the terms were genuinely negotiated.

**Article L.442-1 Code de commerce.** Between commercial parties, subjecting a
partner to obligations creating a significant imbalance can found liability.
Sometimes pleaded alongside 1171.

**Gross negligence / faute lourde.** A limitation clause will not protect a party
guilty of *dol* (fraud) or *faute lourde* - conduct so serious it reveals an
inability to perform the obligation assumed. The threshold is high. Ordinary
slippage, even prolonged, is not usually enough on its own.

**Practical point.** Attacking a cap is a real argument but rarely a quick one.
It is normally worth pricing as a litigation risk rather than assuming it
succeeds.
